# Head & Neck Oncology Digest — September 14, 2026

**For the surgeon · Literature window: September 1–14, 2026**

**17 new DOI/NCT records · 0 repeated sources.** This is a strict delta from the August 31 cycle: 15 newly indexed publications and two newly posted ClinicalTrials.gov protocols. Numeric claims were checked against PubMed-indexed abstracts or open full text; registry details were checked against ClinicalTrials.gov on September 14, 2026.

Each finding includes provenance, methods, exact results or protocol, surgical interpretation, limitations, and a do-not-overread boundary. Registry-only entries are labeled and contain no efficacy claims.

## Highlights — key takeaways

- **Response-adapted surgery gained prospective phase II evidence, not a new standard.** REMATCH reported 90% 2-year DFS and 100% laryngeal preservation among 42 deep responders selected for reduced-volume surgery, but it was single-arm and the five poor responders had markedly worse outcomes.
- **ctDNA performance is impressive but setting-dependent.** Tissue-informed surveillance reached 95.2% per-test sensitivity and 100% specificity in one retrospective cohort; serial ctHPV DNA in 232 baseline-positive patients reached 90.3% sensitivity and 97.9% specificity, while a 20-study meta-analysis produced a lower pooled sensitivity of 80.3%.
- **Reconstruction counseling should separate frailty from age.** Frailty consistently predicted medical morbidity, longer stay, non-home discharge, and slower swallowing recovery, whereas a 206-patient glossectomy cohort found most age-group functional differences resolved by one to two years.
- **Proton therapy reduced acute oral toxicity in randomized postoperative data.** Grade ≥2 mucositis was 7.4% with protons versus 22.7% with IMRT; control and survival did not differ at the available follow-up.
- **Radiotherapy avoidance remains investigational even after a 65.6% pCR.** A 32-patient HPV-associated OPSCC phase II study avoided postoperative RT/CRT in 91%, but missed its primary endpoint and lacks a randomized comparator.
- **Systemic pipeline signals remain early.** Finotonlimab plus SCT200 produced a 56.7% ORR in 30 treatment-naïve patients, while a new ficerafusp/pembrolizumab phase II asks a dosing question and has no outcomes.

**Quiet watchlist:** The search found no new eligible September 1–14 report for KEYNOTE-689, NIVOPOST, petosemtamab, or perioperative/adjuvant head-and-neck cSCC. Those lanes remain on watch rather than being padded with lower-value material.

## 1. Response-adapted surgery moves into prospective testing

**Bottom line:** Three phase II studies deepen the perioperative signal, but only REMATCH prospectively changed the operation according to response. None establishes safe off-protocol resection reduction, surgery omission, or adjuvant de-intensification.

### REMATCH: reduced-volume surgery after deep response

**Provenance/access:** PubMed structured abstract; prospective single-arm phase II.

**Methods/results:** Fifty patients with resectable HPV-negative stage III–IVA HNSCC completed three cycles of penpulimab, nab-paclitaxel, and cisplatin. Imaging and laryngoscopy before cycle 3 assigned patients with ≥50% primary-tumor regression to reduced-volume surgery (RVS); lesser responders received standard-volume surgery. ORR was 94% (95% CI 84%–99%). At 34.0 months' median follow-up, per-protocol 2-year DFS was 83% (69%–91%). RVS patients (n=42) had 90% 2-year DFS and 100% laryngeal preservation in laryngeal/hypopharyngeal cancers; standard-volume patients (n=5) had 20% 2-year DFS. Grade 3 treatment-related events occurred in 16% during neoadjuvant therapy and 15% around surgery; no grade 4–5 events were reported.

**Surgical interpretation:** This is the most direct prospective evidence yet that a prespecified clinical-response rule can operationalize reduced-volume surgery while preserving near-term disease control in selected responders.

**Limitations/do not overread:** Single center, no randomized surgical comparator, only five poor responders, post-treatment selection, and all patients received risk-adapted postoperative RT. The study does not prove noninferiority of RVS or justify changing the planned operation outside a protocol.

**Source:** [Clinical Cancer Research; DOI 10.1158/1078-0432.CCR-26-1337](https://doi.org/10.1158/1078-0432.CCR-26-1337)

### neoCHANCE-2: afatinib added to chemoimmunotherapy

**Provenance/access:** PubMed abstract/open journal record; single-center, open-label, single-arm phase II (NCT05516589).

**Methods/results:** Forty patients received two cycles of tislelizumab plus nab-paclitaxel/cisplatin, with afatinib between cycles, before resection. Thirty-two proceeded to surgery. Among resected patients, pCR was 40.6% (95% CI 23.7%–59.4%); ORR in the treated cohort was 82.5% (67.2%–92.7%). One-year OS was 97.3% (92.2%–100%). Grade 3–4 lymphopenia occurred in 5/40 (12.5%); there were no grade 5 treatment-related events or treatment-related surgical delays.

**Surgical interpretation:** The regimen generated substantial pathologic clearance without an evident surgical-delay signal, supporting comparative development and planned analysis of resection extent and morbidity.

**Limitations/do not overread:** Eight treated patients did not undergo surgery, the pCR denominator is only 32, follow-up is short, and there is no comparator isolating afatinib's contribution. Do not infer survival benefit or operability improvement.

**Source:** [Journal for ImmunoTherapy of Cancer; DOI 10.1136/jitc-2025-014100](https://doi.org/10.1136/jitc-2025-014100)

### Camrelizumab plus apatinib: chemotherapy-free pathologic activity

**Provenance/access:** PubMed structured abstract; exploratory phase II with one- versus two-cycle allocation.

**Methods/results:** Forty-one patients with resectable stage II–IVA HNSCC received one cycle (n=26) or two cycles (n=15) of camrelizumab plus apatinib. All underwent R0 resection without delay. Overall MPR was 29.3% (12/41), numerically 40.0% after two cycles versus 23.1% after one (p=.300). Grade ≥3 treatment-related events occurred in 1/41 (2.4%). MPR was associated with PD-L1 CPS ≥20 (50.0% versus 13.0%; multiplicity-adjusted p=.023) and circulating-tumor-cell clearance (87.5% versus 36.8%; adjusted p=.033).

**Surgical interpretation:** A non-cytotoxic regimen produced measurable pathologic activity with all patients reaching surgery; CPS and CTC observations can inform prospective response-stratification work.

**Limitations/do not overread:** Small exploratory trial, unequal short exposures, no standard-treatment control, underpowered cycle comparison, biomarker multiplicity, and no mature survival outcomes. MPR associations are not validated selection rules.

**Source:** [Clinical Cancer Research; DOI 10.1158/1078-0432.CCR-26-0466](https://doi.org/10.1158/1078-0432.CCR-26-0466)

**Section counterpoint:** Response is now being linked to surgical scope prospectively, but baseline extent, imaging/laryngoscopy, pathology, postoperative treatment, and salvageability are inseparable parts of the observed outcome.

## 2. ctDNA: high surveillance performance, unresolved decision impact

**Bottom line:** Two cohorts and a meta-analysis sharpen expected test performance. They do not show that replacing standard surveillance, accelerating salvage, or omitting therapy based on ctDNA improves outcomes.

### Tissue-informed ctDNA across HPV-positive and HPV-negative HNSCC

**Provenance/access:** PubMed structured abstract; retrospective tertiary-center cohort.

**Methods/results:** A commercial tumor-informed assay was evaluated in 77 pretreatment and 107 surveillance patients. A personalized assay was successfully detected pretreatment in 71/77 (92.2%). In surveillance, per-test sensitivity was 95.2% (20/21; 95% CI 83.3%–100%), specificity 100% (157/157), PPV 100% (20/20), and NPV 99.4% (157/158). Per-patient sensitivity was 94.4% (17/18), specificity 100% (89/89), and NPV 98.9% (89/90). Median time since treatment completion was 18 months.

**Surgical interpretation:** Patient-specific assays may extend ctDNA surveillance beyond virally driven disease and can support prospective salvage-timing and postoperative-risk trials.

**Limitations/do not overread:** Retrospective ordering and follow-up, commercial-assay selection, small numbers of recurrences, and 7.8% pretreatment assay failure. Perfect observed specificity has a finite confidence interval and does not establish a treatment rule.

**Source:** [JAMA Otolaryngology–Head & Neck Surgery; DOI 10.1001/jamaoto.2026.2469](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamaoto.2026.2469)

### Serial ctHPV DNA in 232 baseline-positive patients

**Provenance/access:** PubMed structured abstract; prospective serial surveillance embedded in a single-institution cohort.

**Methods/results:** Investigators performed 2,928 assessments in 232 HPV-associated OPSCC/unknown-primary patients, including 2,192 surveillance tests. Among 220 patients with radiologic and molecular response, ctHPV DNA reappearance had 90.3% sensitivity, 97.9% specificity, 87.5% PPV, and 98.4% NPV for confirmed recurrence. Reappearance preceded clinical recurrence in 58.1% of recurrent cases. Positivity one month after RT-based treatment was associated with worse OS (HR 19.1; 95% CI 7.5–48.8) and DFS (HR 15.4; 6.7–35.4); an immediate post-treatment result had limited prognostic value.

**Surgical interpretation:** The timing result is practical: a one-month value may be more informative than an immediate post-RT sample, and persistent/reappearing signal should prompt protocolized evaluation rather than an automatic operation.

**Limitations/do not overread:** Only patients with detectable baseline ctHPV DNA were eligible; testing used qPCR at one institution; management effects and lead-time benefit were not randomized. High NPV reflects both assay performance and low recurrence prevalence.

**Source:** [Oral Oncology; DOI 10.1016/j.oraloncology.2026.108126](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.oraloncology.2026.108126)

### Meta-analysis calibrates ctHPV DNA expectations downward

**Provenance/access:** PubMed structured abstract; PRISMA systematic review/meta-analysis.

**Methods/results:** Twenty studies met inclusion criteria. Across 3,505 clinically correlated tests, pooled sensitivity was 80.3% (95% CI 70.9%–87.2%) and specificity 95.5% (90.9%–97.8%). At an estimated 9.70% recurrence prevalence, modeled NPV was 97.7% and PPV 66.0%. A positive post-treatment test was associated with 130-fold higher odds of recurrence, with a very wide 95% CI (56.6–298.7).

**Surgical interpretation:** The pooled estimate is a more conservative benchmark than many single-cohort reports and reinforces confirmatory testing and imaging/pathologic correlation before salvage decisions.

**Limitations/do not overread:** Heterogeneous assays, intervals, thresholds, and reference standards; the abstract does not report patient-level time-to-event synthesis. Modeled predictive values will change with local prevalence.

**Source:** [Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery; DOI 10.1002/ohn.70426](https://doi.org/10.1002/ohn.70426)

## 3. Surgery and reconstruction: measure function, not age alone

**Bottom line:** This cycle adds a technical warning for optical margins and useful counseling evidence for free-flap and glossectomy recovery. None supports denying reconstruction or curative surgery on age or a single frailty score.

### Resection changes fluorescence-lifetime signatures by subsite

**Provenance/access:** PubMed abstract/open full text; prospective intraoperative optical study.

**Methods/results:** A fiber-based fluorescence lifetime imaging system measured in-vivo and ex-vivo oral tongue, base-of-tongue, and tonsil tissue in 75 surgical patients. Cancer/noncancer separability improved ex vivo at the base of tongue (index 0.43 versus 0.15 in vivo) but fell after excision in oral tongue (0.18 versus 0.27) and tonsil (0.28 versus 0.46).

**Surgical interpretation:** Margin algorithms cannot be assumed to transfer between in-vivo guidance and ex-vivo specimen assessment; subsite- and workflow-specific validation is necessary.

**Limitations/do not overread:** Diagnostic-separability study, not a randomized margin trial; no evidence that FLIm reduces positive margins, re-resection, recurrence, or operative time.

**Source:** [Journal of Biomedical Optics; DOI 10.1117/1.JBO.31.9.096002](https://doi.org/10.1117/1.JBO.31.9.096002)

### Frailty predicts recovery burden after free-flap reconstruction

**Provenance/access:** PubMed structured abstract; PRISMA systematic review.

**Methods/results:** Eleven studies using validated frailty measures were included; eight were rated good quality by Newcastle–Ottawa criteria. Frailty was consistently associated with more medical complications, longer hospitalization, non-home discharge, impaired swallowing recovery, and longer enteral-nutrition dependence. Associations with surgical complications and short-term mortality were inconsistent. Tools incorporating nutrition or sarcopenia outperformed frailty indices alone for postoperative complications.

**Surgical interpretation:** Frailty is most useful for prehabilitation, discharge planning, and expectation setting—not as a stand-alone contraindication to microvascular reconstruction.

**Limitations/do not overread:** Heterogeneous instruments and outcomes, observational source studies, and no pooled effect estimates in the abstract. A positive screen should trigger fuller assessment rather than automatic treatment de-escalation.

**Source:** [Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery; DOI 10.1002/ohn.70414](https://doi.org/10.1002/ohn.70414)

### Advanced age delays some recovery after glossectomy but not most long-term outcomes

**Provenance/access:** PubMed structured abstract; retrospective cohort.

**Methods/results:** Among 206 oral-tongue cancer patients treated from 2016–2024, 92 were younger than 60, 61 were 60–70, and 53 were older than 70; 109 (53%) had free-flap reconstruction. Older patients had more moderate-to-severe labial range-of-motion and strength deficits at one month. Most age-group functional differences resolved by one to two years, although labial deficits persisted at two years (p=.048). Gastrostomy/tracheostomy dependence, stay, readmission, reoperation, revision, and FACE-Q scores did not differ by age group. Age and glossectomy type independently predicted long-term function.

**Surgical interpretation:** Counsel older patients about a potentially slower early trajectory while avoiding the assumption that age predicts feeding-tube or tracheostomy dependence.

**Limitations/do not overread:** Retrospective selection, single-center rehabilitation patterns, multiple comparisons, and limited detail on defect/reconstruction matching. Comparable observed outcomes may partly reflect selection of fitter older adults.

**Source:** [Journal of Surgical Oncology; DOI 10.1002/jso.70355](https://doi.org/10.1002/jso.70355)

## 4. Radiation: less toxicity is plausible; omission still needs trials

**Bottom line:** Randomized postoperative data favor protons for acute oral toxicity in unilateral fields. RT avoidance after induction therapy and low-neck omission remain promising single-arm or retrospective strategies; a new phase III asks whether hyperfractionation can replace cisplatin.

### Neoadjuvant TPF enabled postoperative RT avoidance in HPV-associated OPSCC

**Provenance/access:** PubMed abstract/open full text; multicenter prospective phase II.

**Methods/results:** Thirty-two eligible patients received three cycles of docetaxel/cisplatin/5-FU followed by transoral surgery. The abstract says 30 underwent transoral surgery but reports R0 resection in 31, an unresolved internal denominator inconsistency. Centrally reviewed pCR was 65.6% and virologic complete response 64.5%; 91% avoided postoperative RT/chemoradiation. Quality-of-life scores recovered to baseline or improved within one year. Twenty-three patients had undetectable ctHPV DNA after chemotherapy.

**Surgical interpretation:** The study shows that a preplanned induction-plus-surgery pathway can spare most selected patients postoperative radiation, making long-term swallowing and control outcomes especially important.

**Limitations/do not overread:** The primary endpoint was not met, cohort was small, there was no control arm, the surgery/R0 denominators conflict in the abstract, and mature survival or recurrence counts are not provided. Do not adopt TPF-driven RT omission outside a trial.

**Source:** [British Journal of Cancer; DOI 10.1038/s41416-026-03610-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41416-026-03610-y)

### Randomized protons versus IMRT for unilateral postoperative treatment

**Provenance/access:** PubMed structured abstract; multicenter randomized phase II (NCT02923570).

**Methods/results:** Ninety-eight analyzable patients with resected, well-lateralized head-and-neck cancers received unilateral adjuvant proton therapy (n=54) or IMRT (n=44), 60 Gy in 30 fractions with 54 Gy elective treatment. Acute grade ≥2 oral mucositis was 7.4% versus 22.7% (p=.03); grade ≥2 dysgeusia was 9.3% versus 31.8% (p=.005). Grade ≥3 dermatitis and grade ≥2 dysphagia were similar. At 27.2 months' median follow-up, late grade 3 events were absent and three-year local control, PFS, and OS did not differ.

**Surgical interpretation:** For patients already selected for unilateral postoperative RT, protons can reduce specific acute oral toxicities; benefit should be weighed against access, plan quality, travel, and cost.

**Limitations/do not overread:** Phase II sample, unequal analyzable groups, wide patient-reported-outcome confidence intervals, uncommon events, and limited power for disease-control equivalence.

**Source:** [Journal of the National Cancer Institute; DOI 10.1093/jnci/djag257](https://doi.org/10.1093/jnci/djag257)

### Contralateral low-neck sparing and feeding-tube use

**Provenance/access:** PubMed structured abstract; retrospective single-institution cohort.

**Methods/results:** Fifty-two p16-positive OPSCC patients with pre/post-treatment swallow studies received contralateral elective upper-neck irradiation (n=28) or whole-neck irradiation (n=24). Esophageal-inlet muscle dose was 29.8 versus 36.1 Gy (p=.011). Feeding-tube placement during/after chemoradiation was 22% versus 57% (p=.006); adjusted one-year hazard ratio was 0.25 (p=.006). Pneumonia, dilation, and recurrence did not differ, and no relapse occurred in the omitted contralateral low neck.

**Surgical interpretation:** The anatomic dose–function relationship is compelling enough for prospective validation and may inform radiation-planning discussion in carefully selected lateralized disease.

**Limitations/do not overread:** Retrospective allocation, only 52 patients, few oncologic events, and no confidence interval reported for the adjusted hazard ratio in the abstract. Absence of observed omitted-neck relapse does not prove safety.

**Source:** [Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery; DOI 10.1002/ohn.70429](https://doi.org/10.1002/ohn.70429)

### HYTC phase III: hyperfractionation versus cisplatin chemoradiation

**Provenance/access:** ClinicalTrials.gov protocol, first posted September 3; recruiting; **no results**.

**Protocol:** International open-label randomized noninferiority trial, planned n=604, comparing 70 Gy/35 fractions over seven weeks with high-dose cisplatin against radiation alone at 81.6 Gy/68 twice-daily fractions over seven weeks. The primary endpoint is seven-year OS; locoregional failure, DFS, distant metastasis, clinician- and patient-reported toxicity, hearing, swallowing-related quality of life, and speech recognition are secondary.

**Surgical interpretation:** If completed, the trial could answer whether intensified fractionation preserves survival while avoiding cisplatin morbidity in locally advanced disease.

**Limitations/do not overread:** No efficacy or safety outcomes; long completion horizon; open-label design; registry detail is insufficient to generalize eligibility or modern systemic-therapy context. Do not substitute hyperfractionation for standard chemoradiation based on registration alone.

**Source:** [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT07801235](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07801235)

## 5. Cutaneous SCC: selective Level IA surgery and late PD-1 toxicity

**Bottom line:** Level IA metastasis was uncommon in a parotid-metastatic cSCC cohort, supporting selective rather than routine inclusion in a clinically negative neck. Longer cemiplimab follow-up found late toxicity uncommon, but the cohort was not head-and-neck-specific.

### Level IA involvement in parotid-region cSCC

**Provenance/access:** PubMed structured abstract; retrospective surgical cohort.

**Methods/results:** Investigators reviewed 131 adults treated with parotidectomy and neck dissection from 2000–2025: 110 had parotid metastases and 21 preauricular cSCC. Level IA disease occurred in 4 patients (3.6%; 95% CI 1%–9%); the occult rate was 0% (95% CI 0%–3.3%). Involvement occurred with higher pathologic nodal burden and more extensive dissection.

**Surgical interpretation:** The data support considering Level IA omission in a clinically negative, lower-burden neck and support the oncologic plausibility of a submental-island flap in carefully selected patients.

**Limitations/do not overread:** Retrospective single-network cohort spanning 25 years, only four events, dissection-selection bias, and wide confidence intervals. High nodal burden should temper omission, and the study does not compare flap strategies.

**Source:** [Head & Neck; DOI 10.1002/hed.70464](https://doi.org/10.1002/hed.70464)

### Cemiplimab: late toxicity in an expanded-access cohort

**Provenance/access:** PubMed structured abstract; retrospective follow-up of the Italian early-access cohort.

**Methods/results:** Among 134 advanced cSCC patients, ORR was 58.9%, disease-control rate 72.4%, median PFS nine months, and median OS 21 months. Fifteen (11.2%) had at least one treatment-related adverse event beginning ≥6 months after therapy; median onset was 12 months (range 6–23), two patients stopped treatment, all events resolved after discontinuation, and none became chronic.

**Surgical interpretation:** Delayed immune toxicity remains relevant during prolonged response and when coordinating salvage or reconstructive surgery; surveillance should not stop at the early treatment window.

**Limitations/do not overread:** Nonrandomized expanded-access population, no head-and-neck-specific subgroup in the abstract, incomplete toxicity granularity, and possible undercapture of delayed events. The response estimate is not a comparative efficacy result.

**Source:** [Journal der Deutschen Dermatologischen Gesellschaft; DOI 10.1111/ddg.70440](https://doi.org/10.1111/ddg.70440)

## 6. Systemic pipeline: PD-1/EGFR activity and a ficerafusp dosing trial

**Bottom line:** Dual PD-1/EGFR blockade produced encouraging response in a small phase Ib cohort. FORTIFI-FLEX is not a registrational efficacy comparison; it randomizes only patients who remain progression-free after a shared 12-week loading regimen to test maintenance dosing.

### Finotonlimab plus SCT200 in recurrent/metastatic HNSCC

**Provenance/access:** PubMed abstract/open full text; prospective multicenter phase Ib (NCT05552807).

**Methods/results:** Forty-one patients received finotonlimab 200 mg every three weeks plus the EGFR antibody SCT200. In 11 patients previously treated with platinum and checkpoint inhibition, ORR was 27.3%, median PFS 5.8 months, and OS 10.6 months. In 30 systemic-therapy-naïve patients, ORR was 56.7%, median PFS 9.6 months, and estimated median OS 13.6 months. Common treatment-related events were hypomagnesemia (63.4%), rash (41.5%), and acneiform dermatitis (36.6%).

**Surgical interpretation:** The response signal may matter for unresectable recurrence or conversion-to-local-therapy research, but it needs a randomized comparator and explicit post-response local-management rules.

**Limitations/do not overread:** Phase Ib, small and heterogeneous cohorts, no control arm, limited confidence-interval reporting in the abstract, and immature survival. Cross-trial comparisons with pembrolizumab/cetuximab regimens are inappropriate.

**Source:** [Cancer Medicine; DOI 10.1002/cam4.72272](https://doi.org/10.1002/cam4.72272)

### FORTIFI-FLEX: maintenance-frequency study after shared ficerafusp loading

**Provenance/access:** ClinicalTrials.gov protocol, first posted September 8; recruiting; **no results**.

**Protocol:** Open-label phase II, planned n=160, for first-line PD-L1 CPS ≥1 recurrent/metastatic HNSCC (HPV-negative if oropharyngeal). Everyone receives ficerafusp alfa 1,500 mg weekly plus pembrolizumab 200 mg every three weeks for 12 weeks. Patients still progression-free then randomize 2:1 to ficerafusp 2,250 mg every three weeks or continued 1,500 mg weekly, both with pembrolizumab. The primary outcome is the proportion alive and progression-free about nine months into the maintenance phase; ORR, PFS, OS, safety, exposure, and response depth are secondary.

**Surgical interpretation:** The study may clarify whether the EGFR/TGF-β bifunctional agent can be delivered less frequently after initial disease control, with practical implications for prolonged therapy burden.

**Limitations/do not overread:** Both groups receive the active combination during loading, only nonprogressors are randomized, and there is no pembrolizumab-alone control. The design cannot establish the combination's incremental efficacy.

**Source:** [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT07807163](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07807163)

## Bottom line for tumor board

This cycle strengthens three conversations without changing standard surgery: prospectively defined response-adapted resection, assay- and time-specific ctDNA surveillance, and treatment planning that measures functional burden. REMATCH is the most surgically provocative result, but its single-arm selection and universal risk-adapted postoperative RT make off-trial resection reduction premature. The most immediately actionable randomized signal is reduced acute mucositis and dysgeusia with protons in unilateral postoperative fields; access and cost still matter. Capture baseline surgical intent before neoadjuvant treatment, document why operative scope changes, measure swallowing and discharge risk rather than using age alone, and treat every registry entry here as a trial opportunity—not evidence of efficacy.
