## Highlights — key takeaways

- **Hard numbers now back the CPS≥10 cutoff argument**: a cost-effectiveness analysis of KEYNOTE-689 published today found perioperative pembrolizumab is cost-effective at CPS>10 (~$134,700/QALY) but wildly not at CPS 1-10 (~$2.18M/QALY) — this is the strongest quantitative support yet for the CPS≥10-only stance flagged last cycle.
- **The "questioning KEYNOTE-689" pile keeps growing**: a new JCO commentary from a Medical College of Wisconsin/Moffitt/UT Southwestern group ("Notably Off Key") adds to the chorus of surgeons and rad oncs pushing back on blanket perioperative IO.
- **De-escalation optimism takes a hit**: a 55-study, ~38,900-patient umbrella review found broad de-intensification strategies in HPV+ OPSCC are associated with *worse* OS, PFS, and locoregional control — a real counterweight to the ORATOR-driven optimism from last cycle.
- **The level IIB omission data got a direct rebuttal**: a letter-and-response exchange in Head & Neck specifically challenges the meta-analysis on selective neck dissection cited last cycle.
- **ctDNA-gated de-escalation is now an actual open trial**, not just a research endpoint: MUSC's OPERATION trial omits postop RT in patients with negative two-week post-op ctHPVDNA — exactly the mechanism flagged as "coming" last cycle.
- **Cutaneous SCC guidelines got a formal 2026 update** (EADO/ESTRO/EORTC, two-part), and new real-world data suggest durable control after as few as 1-2 doses of checkpoint inhibition, without routine surgical consolidation.

## 1. Who gets a medical oncology consult before you book the OR

**[Practice-changing]** A same-day cost-effectiveness analysis gives the CPS≥10 argument real teeth — perioperative pembrolizumab is a reasonable value proposition only above that cutoff.

- University of Michigan modeled the KEYNOTE-689 cohort: for CPS>10, perioperative pembrolizumab produced ~1.35 additional QALYs at an incremental cost of $181,900 (ICER $134,700 — a 57% probability of being cost-effective at a $150,000 willingness-to-pay threshold). For CPS 1-10, the gain was ~0.08 QALYs at an ICER of $2,179,400 (11% probability of cost-effectiveness). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamaoto.2026.2124)

  **Evidence brief — structured abstract; economic model, not a clinical outcome study.** The authors used a three-state partitioned-survival model (event-free, progressed disease, death), digitized KEYNOTE-689 survival curves, US HCUP and Medicare/Medicaid costs, and CheckMate 141 quality-of-life weights. CPS 1-10 was mathematically derived from the published CPS≥1 and CPS≥10 groups. At $150,000/QALY, the modeled probability of cost-effectiveness was 57% for CPS>10 and 11% for CPS 1-10. The result strengthens a CPS-stratified value conversation, but it does not establish CPS 10 as a biological treatment cutoff; immature survival, derived subgroup data, digitized curves, and borrowed utilities all feed the estimate.
- A new JCO commentary, "Notably Off Key," from a multi-institutional radiation oncology/surgery/med onc group, directly questions the clinical benefit of perioperative IO in resectable HNSCC after KEYNOTE-689. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1200/JCO-25-01990)

  **Commentary digest — no new patient cohort.** Seven authors across Medical College of Wisconsin, Moffitt, and UT Southwestern ask whether KEYNOTE-689's EFS signal remains clinically meaningful after extra treatment, immune toxicity, possible delay to definitive surgery, and the burden of carrying therapy through a curative pathway are weighed together. This sharpens informed consent but contributes no independent response, recurrence, survival, toxicity, or surgical-delay estimate.
- A companion editorial in JAMA Otolaryngology discusses logistics of subcutaneous pembrolizumab and how it changes the coordination of neoadjuvant scheduling with surgery. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamaoto.2026.2132)

  **Viewpoint digest — structured abstract; no outcomes.** Miller and Rocco focus on a delivery pathway: selection, dose-to-OR scheduling, ownership of immune-toxicity holds, and closed-loop handoff before surgery. Subcutaneous administration can reduce conventional infusion-chair friction, but the paper does not establish efficacy equivalence in resectable HNSCC, an optimal interval to surgery, or a safety rule for proceeding after toxicity.
- A multidisciplinary expert consensus roadmap (15 international experts) ranked "personalized chemoradiation following perioperative immunotherapy" and "risk-adapted prognostic evaluation at diagnosis" as the top unmet needs in locally-advanced HNSCC — i.e., the field agrees the current one-size-fits-all approach isn't settled. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.radonc.2026.111711)

  **Roadmap digest — expert ranking, not a guideline.** The top priorities expose two linked gaps: which baseline clinical/viral/pathologic/molecular features should assign intensity, and whether postoperative dose, volume, or concurrent therapy should change after response or nonresponse to perioperative IO. It supports planning the whole sequence before treatment starts, but expert weighting is not comparative-effectiveness evidence.

**Counterpoint:** a systematic review of 25 RCTs in resectable LA-HNSCC (industry-funded, Merck) found EFS improvement with perioperative pembrolizumab, adjuvant nivolumab, and several other regimens was broadly comparable to SoC on OS, with pathologic response used as an early surrogate — a reminder that "no OS signal yet" cuts both ways depending on who's reading the same data. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12325-026-03604-5)

**Go deeper:** for a practical, surgeon-authored framework on exactly when to loop in the surgeon across the disease course (upfront, salvage, palliative), see the OHSU review "When should the medical oncologist call the surgeon?" [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.currproblcancer.2026.101300)

## 2. What's replacing your post-op surveillance imaging

**[Practice-changing within trials]** ctDNA-gated de-escalation has moved from "coming" to an actual open protocol, and the specialty society just weighed in formally.

- MUSC's **OPERATION trial** (NCT07513389, recruiting) omits immediate postoperative radiation in patients with intermediate pathologic risk features and a negative two-week post-op ctHPVDNA (NavDx) result — the first trial found that operationalizes exactly the "ctDNA gates adjuvant therapy" mechanism flagged last cycle.

  **Trial brief — live registry; no outcomes yet.** This is a single-arm MUSC phase II study with 36 planned patients and 2-year local-regional control as the primary endpoint. The registered pathway is TORS → two-week postoperative NavDx → omit immediate postoperative RT if ctHPVDNA-negative → standard surveillance plus serial ctHPVDNA. Selected criteria include diffuse p16≥70%, HPV16 TTMV positivity before surgery, cT0-2N1M0 disease, low nodal burden, ≤10 pack-years, ECOG 0-1, and candidacy for TORS. It can establish feasibility and a preliminary control rate in a favorable-risk population, not prove that ctDNA replaces pathology, imaging, or postoperative RT broadly. [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07513389)
- The American Head and Neck Society published its first formal review of ctDNA in JAMA Otolaryngology, addressing test variability and the implications of an inaccurate result — useful as the single best current overview of where the field stands and what's still unresolved. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamaoto.2026.2045)

  **Review digest — AHNS; 11 authors.** The paper separates four jobs that should not be collapsed: diagnosis, treatment-response monitoring, postoperative MRD, and surveillance/treatment adaptation. Viral ctDNA in HPV-associated OPSCC and EBV-associated NPC has the cleanest biological rationale, but assay, time point, prior probability, and intended action must be named. A false negative can support unsafe reassurance or de-escalation; a false positive can trigger imaging, procedures, and anxiety.
- A new customized 11-gene NGS tumor-informed ctDNA assay in HPV-negative locally-advanced HNSCC (n=40) found 74.3% pre-op / 37.1% post-op positivity using tumor-specific variants (vs. 20%/2.5% using pathogenic-variant-only interpretation), and pretreatment positivity predicted worse progression-free survival — a reminder that assay design (tumor-specific vs. pathogenic-variant calling) materially changes detection rates. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijc.70478)

  **Evidence brief — prospective cohort; structured abstract.** The broader tumor-specific strategy found a trackable alteration in 35/35 informative tumors, versus 30/36 using pathogenic variants alone. Crucially, the reported PFS association belonged to pretreatment positivity under the lower-sensitivity pathogenic-variant definition (p=0.025), not to the broader definition used for the headline detection rates. Analytic sensitivity and prognostic discrimination are separate validation problems.
- **SCREEN-HPV** (NCT06528353, not yet recruiting, n=10,000) will test blood-based HPV16-E6 serology plus ctDNA for population-level screening of HPV-driven OPC — a different use case (early detection, not surveillance) worth knowing about if patients ask.

  **Protocol digest — no screening outcomes yet.** The French study plans to screen 10,000 men aged ≥50 with HPV16-E6 serology, anticipating roughly 100 seropositive participants. They enter five years of ctDNA, head-and-neck/anogenital examination, and indicated imaging; the first 500 also provide a ctDNA control sample. If ctDNA remains positive without a visible lesion, prophylactic tonsil/base-of-tongue surgery may be offered. Yield, false positives, morbidity, and patient-reported burden are therefore as important as analytical detection; this study cannot yet show a mortality benefit.

**Counterpoint:** a broader liquid-biopsy systematic review flags persistent heterogeneity in isolation protocols and detection platforms (NGS vs. digital PCR) as the main barrier to clinical implementation — the OPERATION trial is a single-institution, single-assay pilot (n=36), not a validated standard. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/10445498261475085)

**Go deeper:** the AHNS review above is the best single starting point; for methodology depth, the NGS tumor-informed assay paper is the most rigorous primary dataset this cycle.

## 3. Where dissection extent is actually being questioned

**[Hypothesis-generating]** The level IIB omission data from last cycle just got directly contested in print, and a new systematic review offers a cleaner answer for a different subsite.

- A letter to the editor, "Omission of Level IIB Dissection in cN0 HNSCC: Some Caveats!," directly challenges the meta-analysis on selective neck dissection cited last cycle; the original authors published a response defending their conclusions. Worth reading both before changing practice. [Letter DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/hed.70422) / [Response DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/hed.70420)

  **Correspondence digest — no new cohort.** The exchange audits the assumptions between a low pooled level-IIB involvement rate and an operative omission recommendation: heterogeneity by subsite and selection, study design, and the acceptable residual-risk threshold. Neither side adds nodal specimens or recurrence outcomes; read both against the [original meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1002/hed.70393), not as independent confirmation or refutation.
- A cleaner signal comes from early-stage (cT1-2N0) **supraglottic** SCC: a 16-study systematic review found occult nodal metastasis in 13-37.5% of patients, concentrated almost entirely in level II (especially IIA, 75-85%), with levels I, IIB, IV, and V rarely involved (<5%). Elective treatment improved regional control versus observation, while limiting dissection to levels II-III preserved control in lateralized disease—supporting omission of level IV and IIB in this specific subsite. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.suronc.2026.102484)

  **Evidence brief — open-access systematic review.** Elective treatment improved regional control versus observation, while the anatomical distribution consistently favored a narrower II-III field for lateralized early tumors. T2 classification, poor differentiation, and epilaryngeal involvement tracked with higher occult risk. Midline disease may still require bilateral management, and the review did not establish a consistent adjusted overall-survival benefit. [Open article and graphical abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960740426001350)
- A prospective feasibility study of **sentinel lymph node biopsy in gingivo-buccal SCC** (n=39, India) reported 100% sensitivity/specificity for detecting occult nodal disease — small numbers, but a genuine alternative-to-SOND signal worth tracking in a high-incidence population. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/hed.70403)

  **Study digest — prospective feasibility cohort.** Radiocolloid/SPECT, intraoperative gamma-probe mapping, and frozen section were tested against selective neck dissection in every patient. Sentinel nodes were found in all 39 patients (36 on imaging plus surgery; 3 intraoperatively only). Among 746 nodes, 14 were positive; 12 were sentinel nodes across 8 patients, and the two positive non-sentinel nodes occurred in already SLNB-positive necks. Median follow-up was 27 months (36 NED, 3 died of disease). Perfect performance in only eight node-positive patients has wide uncertainty and is not an SLNB-only noninferiority result.
- A large NCDB analysis (n=5,569) used machine-learning risk stratification after transoral surgery in HPV+ OPSCC to identify a low-risk group where adjuvant therapy conferred no measurable OS benefit — refining who can safely skip adjuvant RT/CRT after TORS, pending prospective validation. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.oraloncology.2026.108024)

  **Study digest — retrospective NCDB cohort.** TOS alone accounted for 2,500 patients (44.9%), TOS+RT 1,807 (32.4%), and TOS+CRT 1,262 (22.7%). DeepSurv, neural multi-task logistic regression, and random survival forest models achieved C-indices of 0.77-0.80. DeepSurv found no measurable OS benefit from adjuvant treatment in low risk, RT or CRT benefit in intermediate risk, and the largest CRT benefit in high risk. Age, margin, comorbidity, insurance, and facility volume were influential; ENE importance varied by model. Treatment-selection confounding and OS-only registry outcomes prevent using this as an omission rule.

**Counterpoint:** the SLNB gingivo-buccal series is a single-center pilot of 39 patients — encouraging but not remotely powered to change SOND as the default. The ML risk-stratification study is retrospective (NCDB) with model-dependent effect sizes; the authors themselves call for prospective validation before de-escalating.

**Go deeper:** a systematic review/meta-analysis of TORS vs. definitive chemoradiation for OPSCC (15 studies, 6,767 patients) found better OS with TORS overall (HR 0.51) but no OS advantage specifically in HPV+ disease, and comparable dysphagia outcomes — the best current synthesis of the surgery-vs-nonsurgery question for OPSCC. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/hed.70308)

## 4. Radiation de-escalation — a necessary reality check

**[Background, tempering prior optimism]** The umbrella review this cycle is a genuine counterweight to the ORATOR-driven enthusiasm from last digest.

- An umbrella review of systematic reviews/meta-analyses (accounting for study overlap) found that across 55 studies (n=38,929), broad de-intensification strategies in HPV+ OPSCC were associated with *worse* OS (HR 1.33), PFS (HR 2.11), locoregional control (HR 2.51), and distant control (HR 1.90) versus standard-of-care. Cetuximab substitution for cisplatin specifically showed inferior OS (HR 2.83) and LRC (HR 2.78) without a significant late-toxicity benefit — consistent with, and reinforcing, the RTOG 1016 story from last cycle. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/hed.70340)

  **Evidence brief — umbrella review.** The pooled 95% confidence intervals were 1.17-1.52 for OS, 1.65-2.69 for PFS, 1.75-3.59 for locoregional control, and 1.25-2.90 for distant control. The most concrete warning came from five randomized cetuximab-substitution trials (n=1,560). But drug substitution, radiation dose or volume reduction, and post-TORS omission are not interchangeable; the pooled estimate argues against blanket de-escalation, not every biomarker-selected strategy.
- The same review notes concrete trial-status updates worth knowing: ADEPT closed early, ECOG-E3311 has mature long-term follow-up, and PATHOS phase III is active but no longer recruiting.

  **Trial-horizon digest — three different evidence states.** ADEPT (NCT01687413) terminated for slow accrual and funding with 42 participants, leaving its RT-versus-chemoradiation question underpowered. E3311 reported 359 evaluable patients: overall 54-month PFS 90.6%; PFS was 93.2% with observation, 94.9% with 50 Gy, 90.2% with 60 Gy, and 85.5% in the high-risk chemoradiation arm. All four observation-arm recurrences occurred among N1 patients. PATHOS (NCT02215265) targets 1,269 participants with 12-month MDADI and overall survival as co-primary endpoints; completion is estimated in 2028. [E3311 follow-up](https://doi.org/10.1200/JCO-24-02550) · [ADEPT registry](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01687413) · [PATHOS registry](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02215265)
- A Lancet letter exchange debates how to interpret TORPEdO, the randomized phase III comparison of intensity-modulated proton therapy and IMRT for oropharyngeal cancer. [Letter DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(26)01070-6) / [Reply DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(26)01182-7)

  **Correspondence digest — read against the underlying trial.** TORPEdO randomized 205 patients across 20 UK hospitals to IMPT or IMRT, both with 70 Gy/33 fractions and high-dose cisplatin. At 12 months, UW-QoL physical scores were 78.3 vs 77.1 (difference 1.3; p=0.56) and MDADI scores 79.5 vs 79.7; 24-month locoregional control was 94% vs 97% and OS 95% vs 95%. The trial found no measured functional or disease-control advantage, while the exchange probes generalizability, unmeasured/late effects, and individual selection. “More conformal” should not automatically be translated into “clinically better.” [TORPEdO](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(26)00314-4)

**Counterpoint:** the umbrella review itself cautions that "de-intensification" is a heterogeneous bucket — some approaches (e.g., ctDNA-gated RT omission, discussed in Section 2) weren't powered in this pooled analysis and may behave differently than blanket dose reduction or drug substitution. Don't read this as "de-escalation is dead," but do read it as "de-escalation needs a selection biomarker, not just HPV status."

**Go deeper:** the umbrella review is the single best current synthesis — it explicitly re-analyzes overlap across prior meta-analyses rather than double-counting the same trials.

## 5. Cutaneous SCC: guidelines catch up, dosing questions open up

**[Practice-changing]** The European cSCC guidelines got a formal two-part 2026 update, and new real-world data suggest less immunotherapy may be enough for responders.

- EADO/ESTRO/EORTC published an updated two-part consensus guideline (diagnostics/prevention, and treatment) for invasive cSCC — first-line remains surgical excision or micrographic surgery for resectable disease, cemiplimab remains the EMA-approved first-line systemic option for unresectable/metastatic disease, and adjuvant cemiplimab after surgery+RT in high-risk disease should go to tumor board discussion. [Part 1 DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejca.2026.116763) / [Part 2 DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejca.2026.116764)

  **Guideline digest — 2026 European consensus.** Part 1 adopts five difficult-to-treat groups: (1) common cSCC made complex by tumor/patient factors, (2) multiplicity, (3) locally advanced disease, (4) regional nodal/cutaneous metastases, and (5) distant metastases. Its diagnostic sequence is clinical/dermoscopic assessment with histologic confirmation → examination/imaging when indicated → integration into AJCC8/BWH staging. Part 2 keeps clear-margin surgery first-line for common disease, anti-PD-1 first-line systemically for advanced disease when indicated, and multidisciplinary selection for adjuvant cemiplimab. The classification organizes care but is not a validated prognostic model.
- A Bayesian causal analysis from Mass Eye and Ear/MGH (the **FIRST** study, n=189) found nearly half of patients received ≤2 doses of checkpoint inhibition, and among 50 complete responders managed without surgery, only 1 recurrence occurred over a median 25.4-month follow-up — suggesting durable control can be achieved without routine surgical consolidation or fixed-duration neoadjuvant courses. Benefit was concentrated early (2 vs. 1 dose), with uncertain incremental value beyond 2 doses. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/jitc-2026-015029)

  **Full-text evidence brief — retrospective cohort; open access.** Objective response was 63% (119/189), clinical complete response 27.5% (52/189), and 48.7% (92/189) received ≤2 doses. The largest modeled gain appeared between one and two doses; additional exposure was less certain. Response was clinician-anchored rather than RECIST-based, surgery was omitted selectively, and dose count was not randomized, so the model is not a dosing recommendation. [Open full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13386037/) · [Dose-response source figure](https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/d857/13386037/5d2eee5a53b6/jitc-14-7-g005.jpg) (Miller et al., JITC 2026; unmodified, [CC BY-NC 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/4.0/))
- A second international collaborative case series (n=112) benchmarks outcomes for craniofacial resection in advanced anterior skull base cSCC: 5-year OS 52.3%, with intracranial extension the strongest independent predictor of worse survival (HR 6.54) — useful as a modern surgical benchmark against which to judge whether an immunotherapy-first approach is paying off in your own patients. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/hed.70412)

  **Study digest — retrospective international series, 1995-2015.** The cohort was 86.6% T4 and 71.4% recurrent; margins were clear in 32.1% and involved in 40.2%. Five-year DFS/DSS/OS were 46.9%/68.4%/52.3%. Intracranial extension carried OS HR 6.54 and DSS HR 12.39; adjuvant therapy, usually postoperative RT, was associated with better OS (HR 0.54). Complications occurred in 38.4% and perioperative mortality was 4.5%. This is a high-morbidity pre-immunotherapy benchmark, not a counterfactual comparison with PD-1 therapy.
- HLX07, a novel anti-EGFR antibody, showed a 60% ORR at the 1000mg dose level (vs. 19% at the higher 1500mg dose) in a phase II trial of advanced cSCC — an emerging non-IO option worth knowing about for IO-ineligible or IO-refractory patients, though the inverse dose-response relationship is unexplained and needs a larger trial to sort out. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/17588359261456684)

  **Trial digest — open-label phase II Part 1; n=31.** In the 1500 mg (n=21) vs 1000 mg (n=10) cohorts, independent-review ORR was 19.0% vs 60.0%, median PFS 4.9 vs 7.9 months, median response duration 5.0 vs 7.4 months, and grade ≥3 treatment-related adverse events 38.1% vs 30.0%; no treatment-related death or discontinuation was reported. The cohorts were small and nonrandomized, so imbalance can easily create the inverse dose-response and the ten-patient confidence interval is wide.

**Counterpoint:** the FIRST study is retrospective and real-world (not a randomized dose-finding trial); "≤2 doses may be enough" is a hypothesis worth testing prospectively, not a protocol to adopt today. The craniofacial resection series is a historical cohort (1995-2015) that predates routine immunotherapy access, so it's a benchmark, not a comparator.

**Go deeper:** the EADO/ESTRO/EORTC Part 2 guideline is the most current, broadly-endorsed treatment algorithm; last cycle's Kreft et al. review (already covered, not re-summarized here) remains the best discussion of the neoadjuvant-vs-surgery-first sequencing debate.

## 6. Systemic pipeline for recurrent/metastatic disease

**[Background/pipeline]** Several new trials are testing next-generation ADCs and bispecifics in combination with checkpoint inhibition, largely in the neoadjuvant/1L R-M space.

- AbbVie's telisotuzumab adizutecan (a c-Met-directed ADC) + pembrolizumab is entering a randomized phase II as first-line therapy for recurrent/metastatic HNSCC (NCT07744126, not yet recruiting, n=150).

  **Protocol brief — live registry; no results.** The three planned arms are telisotuzumab adizutecan dose A + pembrolizumab, dose B + pembrolizumab, and pembrolizumab + platinum + 5-FU as the active comparator. Primary outcomes include safety, blinded-independent-review ORR, and PFS under RECIST 1.1; duration of response and overall survival are secondary. The estimated start is September 2026 and completion October 2029. This maps the future comparison but supports no efficacy inference today. [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07744126)
- Becotatug vedotin (MRG003, an EGFR-directed ADC) combined with PD-1 inhibitors (toripalimab or pucotenlimab) is being tested in three separate Chinese trials spanning neoadjuvant PD-L1+ resectable disease, locally recurrent resectable disease, and hypopharyngeal SCC specifically (NCT07677475, NCT07632339, NCT07665190) — an EGFR-ADC-plus-IO class worth watching alongside ficerafusp alfa and petosemtamab.

  **Program digest — three registries, no results.** NCT07677475 randomizes 65 PD-L1 CPS≥1 resectable LA-HNSCC patients 2:1 to two cycles of MRG003+toripalimab or toripalimab before surgery (primary endpoint MPR). BLOOM-ON/NCT07632339 randomizes 102 locally recurrent resectable patients to two cycles MRG003+pucotenlimab before surgery or upfront surgery (primary one-year EFS rate). NCT07665190 is a 52-patient single-arm hypopharyngeal study of three preoperative cycles (primary pCR). The combined planned enrollment is 219, but MPR, pCR, and EFS across different settings cannot be compared as a leaderboard. [NCT07677475](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07677475) · [NCT07632339](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07632339) · [NCT07665190](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07665190)
- University of Chicago is testing induction cemiplimab + chemotherapy with or without fianlimab (a LAG-3 inhibitor) in locoregionally advanced HNSCC (NCT07346196) — the first LAG-3 combination we've flagged in this space.

  **Protocol digest — randomized phase II; n=90; no outcomes.** The variable is fianlimab during three nine-week induction cycles; both arms receive cemiplimab+carboplatin/paclitaxel, then seven weeks of cisplatin chemoradiation to 70 Gy, followed by six months of cemiplimab. The primary nine-week ORR uses a pick-the-winner design; survival, disease control, and acute/late toxicity are secondary. The estimated start is 2027 and completion 2031, so the registry supports no efficacy claim today. [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07346196)
- A review from an Italian/European group provides a comprehensive current synthesis of immunotherapeutic strategies across HPV+ and HPV- recurrent/metastatic OPSCC, covering PD-1 blockade, EGFR-directed bispecifics (ficerafusp alfa, petosemtamab, amivantamab), and therapeutic HPV vaccines targeting E6/E7 — the best single overview if you want the full landscape in one place. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.critrevonc.2026.105378)

  **Landscape digest — narrative review; 10 authors; 2000-Feb 2026 search.** HPV-positive OPSCC is framed as more antigen-rich and immune-inflamed; HPV-negative disease more often shows immune exclusion, EGFR dependence, hypoxia, and MET/HGF or TGF-β activation. HPV status is still prognostic rather than a validated predictive marker for checkpoint benefit. The review maps vaccines and PD-1 combinations for HPV-positive disease and agents such as ficerafusp alfa, petosemtamab, and amivantamab for HPV-negative resistance biology. It is an orientation map; efficacy numbers belong to the underlying trials.
- A systematic review of HPV therapeutic vaccine preclinical data notes there are still no FDA-approved therapeutic vaccines for HPV-associated cancers, despite two decades of prophylactic vaccine availability — useful context for managing patient expectations when this comes up in clinic. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10555-026-10365-8)

  **Systematic-review digest — preclinical HNSCC models only.** The eight-author review examines antigen, delivery platform, adjuvant, and combination partner. Preventive L1-based vaccines stop infection; therapeutic candidates target persistently expressed tumor antigens such as E6/E7 to generate cellular antitumor immunity. DNA, RNA, peptide, viral-vector, and nanoparticle strategies often pair with checkpoint blockade, radiation, or immune agonists. Murine regression and immune readouts do not establish human response, durability, toxicity, or benefit over PD-1 therapy.

**Go deeper:** the Di Bello et al. review above is the single best current landscape summary; none of the new trials above have efficacy data yet — this section is purely "what to watch," not "what changed."

## Bottom line for tumor board

The CPS≥10 argument that closed out last cycle's digest just picked up hard economic backing — a same-day cost-effectiveness analysis shows perioperative pembrolizumab is a reasonable value proposition above that cutoff and a poor one below it, while yet another commentary questions the whole KEYNOTE-689 premise. At the same time, this cycle is a genuine correction on the de-escalation side: a large umbrella review shows blanket HPV+ de-intensification underperforms standard of care, and the level IIB neck dissection omission data got directly contested in print. The throughline is the same as last time — the field is converging on "de-escalate/intensify by biomarker, not by disease category" — but ctDNA is now closer to doing that job for real, with an actual trial (OPERATION) omitting postop RT based on a negative post-op liquid biopsy rather than just proposing to. Nothing here should change what you do in the OR this month, but the CPS-stratified consult conversation and the "which de-escalation trial is my patient eligible for" conversation both got more concrete.
